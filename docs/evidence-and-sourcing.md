# Evidence and sourcing

## Evidence labels

Use these labels when a material statement could otherwise be mistaken for a stronger claim:

- **Documented fact:** a source directly establishes the event or result described.
- **Attributed testimony:** an identifiable person reported an experience or made a statement. Keep the speaker and limits visible.
- **Reported allegation:** a claim reported by a source but not independently established.
- **Editorial inference:** an interpretation based on stated facts. Explain the reasoning and name plausible alternatives where they matter.
- **Unknown:** available evidence does not resolve the question.

A source can support one claim and be weak for another. A company announcement establishes what it announced; it does not independently validate its explanation or prove that a stated plan was implemented. A registry establishes the information recorded there, not necessarily the complete clinical interpretation. A scientific paper establishes what its authors measured and reported, not an unmeasured mechanism or a general claim about an entire treatment class.

## Documentary source tiers

Assign tiers by the source's documentary role for the specific claim, not by prestige. The tier is a guide, not a mechanical ranking.

1. **Tier 1 — Primary official documentary evidence.** FDA decisions and databases, official trial registries and results postings, SEC filings, court records, corporate registries, insolvency notices, patent-office records, official correspondence, original protocols/statistical analysis plans and other official institutional records. Give these priority for matters within the issuing body's authority: legal status, regulatory action, registration, recorded trial status and endpoints, dates, and documented transactions. State only what the document establishes.
2. **Tier 2 — Primary scientific publications.** Original peer-reviewed trials, prospective clinical studies, feasibility studies and clinical outcome reports. Use these for design, sample, endpoints, statistical results, adverse events, safety, efficacy and limitations. A secondary scientific review article is a synthesis, not a primary results report; label it as such. A clinical publication does not establish company solvency, commercial continuity or device support unless it documents those matters.
3. **Tier 3 — Official corporate and institutional communications.** Press releases, investor communications, official websites, manufacturer notices and acquisition announcements. They establish the organization's public statements, announced plans, stated intentions, product positioning and declared availability. Separate announced intention from a completed, documented transfer and from an operationally implemented transition. A promise of continued support is not evidence that support was delivered.
4. **Tier 4 — Established journalism and specialist reporting.** Financial and medical-technology journalism and reputable investigative reporting. These can support chronology, interviews and reported events, especially where records are unavailable. Identify what is direct documentary evidence and what is journalistically reported, and trace material claims to underlying records when possible.
5. **Tier 5 — Secondary databases and aggregators.** Commercial company databases, business-intelligence platforms, trial aggregators and historical snapshots. Use for discovery, corroboration and context; do not present them as equivalent to official legal or regulatory records. Identify limitations when a significant claim depends on them.
6. **Tier 6 — Anecdotal, testimonial and informal sources.** Patient accounts, clinician statements, forums, blogs, social media and informal reports. They can document an attributed individual experience or identify a possible support issue, but cannot establish population-wide experience without corroboration.

These tiers do not rank every source for every question. Consider proximity to the event, authority over the subject, documentary specificity, date, methodological transparency, directness and corroboration. For example, a registry is usually best for a recorded trial status, while the publication is more informative about clinical outcome interpretation; a court order is more authoritative than a news report on a formal sale; and a company announcement directly supports what the company said, not whether it occurred as planned. Secondary scientific reviews are identified by publication type and are used for synthesis/context rather than being mislabeled as original trials or journalism.

## Claim-level traceability

Keep citations adjacent to the claim and identify source IDs in the dossier evidence register. For material disputed or high-impact claims, use a compact claim-to-source record with: claim ID; exact proposition; source ID and tier; evidence type (direct, indirect or attributed); verification status (confirmed, partially supported, unsupported or unresolved); limitation; and action (retain, rewrite, qualify or remove). Do not duplicate every sentence in a separate matrix when the dossier's evidence register already provides equivalent traceability. Do not mark a source verified merely because its URL is listed.

## Source registers

Each substantive case has a compact register linking source IDs to a claim, source type and limitation. `sources/source-register.md` consolidates the bibliography and describes each source's evidentiary role. A publication date is left as “not displayed” if it could not be verified. Access dates record checks actually performed; they do not mean every source was exhaustively checked or that every URL remains accessible. If a source cannot be opened, record that access limitation and use an accessible primary record where possible. Never invent source contents, quotations, dates or URLs.

## Conflicts, missing evidence and precise language

Record conflicts rather than smoothing them over. Distinguish a company promise, a patient's later experience and a court asset-sale order. Do not infer patient abandonment from company closure, product discontinuation, transfer of intellectual property, asset sale or trial termination. Check device operation, component availability, maintenance, software, clinical follow-up, formal successor responsibilities and reported patient experience separately. Where evidence does not answer these questions, say unknown.

For trials, distinguish termination, early stopping, failure to meet a primary endpoint, lack of statistical significance, absent registry results, absent publication, failure to demonstrate efficacy and demonstrated absence of efficacy. Verify sample, randomization, endpoints and follow-up; distinguish prespecified primary outcomes from secondary, exploratory and post-hoc findings. A result from one study does not establish the effectiveness of a treatment modality as a whole.

## Access dates and link checks

The local validator checks source identifiers and repository structure, not external website availability or factual support. Network failures are not evidence that a URL is invalid. If a source was only visible through an index or excerpt, say so and do not imply that the full document was inspected.
