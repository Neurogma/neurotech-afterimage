## Case identity and outcome

This randomized, placebo-controlled crossover trial studied burst spinal cord stimulation (SCS) in people with chronic radicular pain after lumbar spine surgery. The precise outcome is **no statistically significant superiority over placebo stimulation on the prespecified disability outcome** in this trial and population. It does not establish that every SCS waveform or every pain indication is ineffective.

## What the public record shows

The registry and peer-reviewed report describe a randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled crossover trial of 50 people with chronic radicular pain after lumbar surgery. The primary Oswestry Disability Index contrast favored burst stimulation by only 1.3 points versus placebo (95% CI −3.9 to 1.3; P=.32), and no prespecified secondary outcome differed significantly. The paper also recorded adverse events and surgical revisions after implantation, so both efficacy uncertainty and procedure burden belong in the interpretation. [S-SCS-01] [S-SCS-02] [S-SCS-03]

### What was tested

NCT03546738 was a randomized placebo-controlled crossover study. Fifty participants were randomized to a sequence of two three-month active burst-stimulation periods and two three-month placebo-stimulation periods. The primary outcome was the between-condition difference in Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) change from baseline at 12 months. [S-SCS-01] [S-SCS-02]

### Primary result and follow-up

- **Main trial, 2022.** Forty-seven participants had at least one follow-up ODI assessment; 42 completed all stimulation periods and ODI assessments. Mean ODI change was −10.6 during active stimulation and −9.3 during placebo stimulation. The between-condition difference was −1.3 points (95% CI −3.9 to 1.3; P=.32). The study identified 10 ODI points as the minimal clinically important difference, making the observed mean contrast small relative to that benchmark. None of the prespecified secondary outcomes differed significantly. [S-SCS-02]
- **Procedure-related adverse events.** Nine participants (18%) had adverse events within three months of implantation, and four (8%) required surgical revision. The publication reports events including dural puncture, lead revision, infection and pulse-generator replacement. These events concern the trial’s implanted system and procedures; they should not be generalized to every SCS system or all implanting centres. [S-SCS-02]
- **Six-month post-trial follow-up, 2023.** In a post-trial comparison, ODI was 34.7 at follow-up and 35.4 during placebo; the difference was −0.7 points (95% CI −4.5 to 3.2; P=.73). The trial therefore did not show a meaningful separation even after participants and clinicians were no longer blinded and stimulation could be adjusted. Four participants had the stimulator removed because of no perceived effect or discomfort, and one underwent revision for lead migration. [S-SCS-03]

## What was assumed

The available publications describe the protocol, not every assumption discussed by its designers. **Editorial inference:** a stimulation effect might be presumed from uncontrolled improvement after implantation; the sham-controlled design tested that inference and found little between-condition separation on the prespecified disability scale. This does not prove no individual could benefit, but subjective improvement during treatment alone would not establish causal efficacy.

## What the evidence suggests future teams should do differently

**Editorial inference:** implanting a device creates procedural risks, so a blinded control is especially valuable when expectation and patient-reported outcomes may influence interpretation. The negative primary comparison should be reported directly. Limitations of a trial design can inform future protocol improvements, but they do not erase the actual null result or independently establish efficacy.

For future stimulation studies, prespecify a clinically important effect threshold, track device/procedure harms separately from stimulation effects, preserve blinding where feasible, and report all randomized participants alongside completer analyses. If patient-controlled programming is restricted for blinding, discuss that constraint explicitly while still presenting the placebo-controlled comparison as tested.

## Consequences

The evidence supports no significant superiority of burst stimulation over placebo on the ODI primary outcome for the studied post-surgical radicular pain population, including at post-trial follow-up. It does not prove zero effect, because confidence intervals allow a range of small benefits and harms. Nor does it answer whether other waveforms, indications or selection criteria produce different results.

## What remains unknown

- Whether a prospectively defined subgroup might benefit and whether such a subgroup would replicate in an independent trial.
- Which protocol design best balances blinding with the usual clinical practice of individualized programming.
- Long-term device-related outcomes beyond the reported follow-up.

## Questions future teams should ask

- Is the primary endpoint prespecified and clinically interpretable, not only statistically convenient?
- Are implant complications presented with clear denominators and timelines?
- Does the trial compare a clinically optimized intervention with credible placebo, and what does that choice constrain?
- Are conclusions kept specific to the waveform, population, comparator and endpoint actually tested?
