## Case identity and outcome

ACT2 tested non-invasive vagus nerve stimulation (nVNS) as an acute treatment for cluster-headache attacks. The outcome should be described precisely: the overall primary endpoint comparison did not show a statistically significant benefit, while the episodic cluster-headache subgroup showed a positive signal and the chronic subgroup did not. The study does not justify a blanket conclusion for all headache patients or for all uses of nVNS.

## What the public record shows

The public record combines the registered randomized, sham-controlled study with its peer-reviewed outcome report. In the full analysis set, the pooled primary outcome was 14% of treated attacks pain-free at 15 minutes with nVNS and 12% with sham, without a statistically significant difference; the episodic subgroup showed a positive signal (48% versus 6%), while the chronic subgroup did not (5% versus 13%). The evidence therefore supports a null overall comparison plus a phenotype-specific signal, not a uniform effect across cluster headache. [S-ACT-01] [S-ACT-02]

### What was tested

The randomized, sham-controlled study assigned participants to nVNS or sham after a one-week run-in and used a two-week blinded treatment period. Its primary endpoint was the proportion of treated attacks that were pain-free within 15 minutes without rescue medication. Results were analysed by episodic cluster headache (eCH) and chronic cluster headache (cCH) as well as in the combined population. [S-ACT-01] [S-ACT-02]

### Results

- **Analysis population.** The published full analysis set included 48 participants receiving nVNS and 44 receiving sham. The combined-population primary endpoint was 14% of attacks with nVNS versus 12% with sham; the difference was not statistically significant. [S-ACT-02]
- **Episodic subgroup.** The reported attack-level pain-free proportion was 48% with nVNS and 6% with sham, with a reported p value below .01. This is a subgroup-specific finding and should be interpreted in the context of subgroup size, endpoint unit (treated attack rather than independent person) and the study’s prespecification. [S-ACT-02]
- **Chronic subgroup.** The corresponding values were 5% with nVNS and 13% with sham, without evidence of benefit in that subgroup. The opposite-looking subgroup results argue against averaging across phenotypes and then claiming a uniform response. [S-ACT-02]

## What was assumed

The reviewed public sources do not expose every design deliberation, so no unreported rationale is attributed to investigators. **Editorial inference:** an average effect across episodic and chronic cluster headache may be assumed to represent each phenotype. The reported divergence between subgroups warns against that shortcut, while the episodic signal itself still requires careful analysis and replication before it supports a broad treatment claim.

## What the evidence suggests future teams should do differently

**Editorial inference:** headache phenotype can be a clinically important effect modifier. An overall null result can coexist with a promising subgroup signal, but the subgroup finding needs clear prespecification, adequate power and independent replication before it becomes a general treatment claim. Attack-level outcomes need statistical methods that account for multiple attacks contributed by the same participant.

Trials should state whether the primary estimand is per-person or per-attack, how repeated observations are modelled, and whether episodic/chronic subgroup comparisons were prespecified. Teams should not hide the overall endpoint result, and should not erase a potentially useful subgroup signal simply because the combined estimate is null.

## Consequences

The trial supports a non-significant overall primary comparison in the tested two-week acute-treatment setting and a positive episodic subgroup result, with no analogous chronic-subgroup result. It does not by itself establish long-term prevention efficacy, benefit across all headache conditions, or an explanation for all between-subgroup differences.

## What remains unknown

- The stability of the episodic subgroup effect in larger independent studies.
- The extent to which attack-level repeated-measure modelling changes the uncertainty around the reported subgroup result.
- Whether the same findings generalize to prophylactic use, different stimulation protocols or patients outside the study criteria.

## Questions future teams should ask

- Is a phenotype-specific analysis prespecified and adequately powered?
- Are repeated attacks treated as correlated observations in analysis and reporting?
- Is the 15-minute attack-level endpoint meaningful to patients and linked to rescue medication use?
- Do conclusions clearly distinguish the overall trial result from subgroup results?
