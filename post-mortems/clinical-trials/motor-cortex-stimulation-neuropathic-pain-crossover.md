## Case identity and outcome

Radic et al. reported a prospective multicentre randomized crossover trial of invasive motor cortex stimulation for refractory neuropathic pain. The 12-subject study was stopped early for lack of efficacy and did not demonstrate a significant visual analogue scale pain-score difference between the compared stimulation settings. The small sample and early withdrawals limit interpretation. [S-MCS-01]

## Evidence classification

**Documented fact:** The trial report/abstract supports the specific study design and outcome described above. **Editorial inference:** The institutional lesson is an interpretation, not a claim made directly by the study. **Unknown:** Protocol/registry reconciliation and/or full-text limits are stated below.

## What was built

The intervention involved implanted stimulation of the motor cortex for refractory neuropathic pain. This dossier covers the specific 2015 crossover trial, not all motor cortex stimulation research or every neuropathic pain indication.

## What the public record shows

The paper reports 12 subjects, early termination for lack of efficacy, no significant VAS difference between stimulation settings, and several early withdrawals, including withdrawals associated with transient adverse events and a protocol violation. [S-MCS-01] A separate 2021 randomized study reported some responders; it is a distinct study and is included as a caution against generalizing this trial's outcome to the whole intervention class. [S-MCS-02]

## What was assumed

No claim is made that motor cortex stimulation is universally ineffective, that the trial establishes a general safety rate, or that the implanted hardware's long-term support was discontinued.

## Consequences

The study is relevant to evidence appraisal for invasive neuromodulation: early stopping, small samples, withdrawals, heterogeneous pain presentations and setting comparisons can constrain the certainty of efficacy estimates.

## What remains unknown

A registry identifier for the 2015 trial was not verified in the sources used for this draft. The trial's small size limits generalizability. Current hardware support and maintenance responsibilities are not established by the cited efficacy papers.

## What the evidence suggests future teams should do differently

Plan adequate sample sizes and transparent stopping rules; report withdrawals and adverse events in context; avoid treating one small study as a definitive verdict on an intervention class.

## Questions future teams should ask

- What were the prespecified stopping rules and primary analysis?
- How did withdrawals affect the analyzable sample and uncertainty?
- Are pain diagnoses and stimulation protocols sufficiently comparable across studies?
- Who maintains implanted hardware if the clinical research program ends?
