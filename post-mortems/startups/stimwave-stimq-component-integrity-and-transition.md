## Case identity and outcome

This dossier concerns Stimwave Technologies and the StimQ Peripheral Nerve Stimulator System, while distinguishing the StimQ product issue from other Stimwave product lines. The public record supports a product-component/labeling issue, a later criminal proceeding concerning a fake component implanted in patients, and an organizational transition. It does not establish that all Stimwave products were affected or that all implanted patients lost support.

**Documented fact:** FDA's recall record describes a non-functional component not referenced in product labeling. The U.S. Department of Justice later reported the former CEO's sentencing for conduct involving a fake component implanted in patients. [S-STIM-01] [S-STIM-02]

**Documented fact:** Federal announcements and successor-facing company material document a Chapter 11 / asset-transition context. The existence of a transaction does not, by itself, establish transfer of every patient-support obligation. [S-STIM-03] [S-STIM-04]

## What was built

Stimwave developed peripheral nerve stimulation and spinal cord stimulation products. The relevant recall record specifically names the StimQ Peripheral Nerve Stimulator System; claims about this product must not be generalized to Freedom PNS/SCS or every device associated with the company.

## What the public record shows

- FDA recall record Z-2930-2020 describes the StimQ issue and its labeling context. [S-STIM-01]
- DOJ reported a six-year prison sentence for the former CEO in June 2024, following a jury conviction involving a fake component implanted in patients. The precise legal allegations and adjudicated conduct should be quoted from the DOJ record, not paraphrased into a broader claim about every device. [S-STIM-02]
- Federal and company sources document organizational/legal transition context. Public support material associated with the successor is specific to Freedom PNS/SCS devices and is not sufficient to establish StimQ support arrangements. [S-STIM-03] [S-STIM-04]

## What was assumed

The dossier does not assume that the corporate transition caused a support interruption, that all recipients experienced harm, or that every product shared the recalled component issue.

## Consequences

The documented record raises institutional questions about component verification, labeling, clinician/patient communication, product-level recall scope, and continuity obligations after asset transitions. These are questions for investigation, not claims that a particular downstream consequence occurred in every case.

## What remains unknown

- The number and characteristics of patients affected by the specific component issue and the extent of patient-level outcomes.
- Complete model-specific support arrangements for StimQ recipients after the organizational transition.
- Which contracts, service functions, records, warranties, and clinical responsibilities transferred, and to which entity.
- Whether support differed by device model, clinic, geography, or implant date.

## What the evidence suggests future teams should do differently

Maintain a product/model-level map of components, labeling, corrective actions, implanted recipients, and responsible legal entities. During an asset sale, document explicitly which support functions and clinical obligations transfer; do not infer them from brand continuity or IP ownership.

## Questions future teams should ask

- Which exact models and component lots were affected by each corrective action?
- What did the recall notice require, and what implementation evidence exists?
- Which entity is responsible for each legacy device model after an asset sale?
- Are patient contact, replacement parts, programming, and clinical escalation obligations documented separately?
