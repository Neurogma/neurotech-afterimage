## Case identity and outcome

Pixium Vision SA was a French bioelectronics company developing artificial-vision systems. Its PRIMA programme used a wireless photovoltaic subretinal implant intended to restore limited central visual perception for people with retinal degeneration, including geographic atrophy associated with age-related macular degeneration. In early 2024, Pixium entered judicial liquidation after an attempted sale plan was rejected. Science Corporation subsequently acquired PRIMA-related assets and intellectual property, and the clinical programme continued under the new sponsor. This case is therefore a **corporate failure with technology-programme continuity**, not a demonstrated clinical failure or proof of universal patient abandonment. [S-PX-01]–[S-PX-05]

## What the public record shows

**Documented fact:** Pixium announced that its proceedings progressed from *sauvegarde* to *redressement judiciaire* in November 2023 because sufficient financing had not been secured. In February 2024, the company announced that the Paris Commercial Court had rejected the proposed sale plan and ordered judicial liquidation. [S-PX-01] [S-PX-02]

Science Corporation announced in April 2024 that it had acquired PRIMA-related intellectual property and assets through the liquidation process and intended to continue development. ClinicalTrials.gov subsequently listed Science as sponsor of PRIMAvera (NCT04676854), with the study active but no longer recruiting in its June 2026 update. An asset transfer and a successor-sponsored trial demonstrate continuity of the programme; they do not, on their own, prove that every legacy contract, warranty, clinical-site relationship or duty to an individual participant transferred unchanged. [S-PX-03] [S-PX-04]

The PRIMAvera study later reported a clinical result. In the prospective, open-label, single-group study, 38 participants received an implant and 32 completed the 12-month assessment; 26 of those 32 (81%) had the specified clinically meaningful improvement in visual acuity from baseline. The report recorded 26 serious adverse events in 19 participants, most occurring within two months after surgery. These results concern the programme under subsequent development; they do not erase the earlier company's financial failure or demonstrate that every legacy responsibility was transferred. [S-PX-05]

## What was built

PRIMA is a photovoltaic subretinal prosthesis rather than a cortical implant. External glasses project an infrared image onto a subretinal microchip, whose pixels convert light into electrical stimulation of remaining retinal cells. Its intended benefit is limited visual function, especially central vision, not restoration of normal sight. At the point of the cited clinical evidence, the programme remained under clinical development; the study result should not be read as proof of broad commercial availability. [S-PX-03] [S-PX-04] [S-PX-05]

## What was assumed

No source cited attributes Pixium's liquidation to a technical failure of PRIMA. The company reported financial and funding problems; the later study reports clinical outcomes from continued development. **Editorial inference:** a programme can survive a company if intellectual property, regulatory files, trial sponsorship and funds are transferred in an organized way. This remains different from proving that all device-support or participant obligations were assumed by the buyer.

## Consequences

The public record establishes liquidation, a later asset/IP transaction and continued clinical development. It does not establish that every trial site or participant experienced seamless continuity, nor does it document total abandonment. Clinical benefit and serious adverse events are both present in the later PRIMAvera result and should be reported together when describing the technology. [S-PX-02]–[S-PX-05]

## What the evidence suggests future teams should do differently

**Editorial inference:** implant programmes should plan for corporate transition at the level of duties, not just intellectual property. Plans should identify the custodian of trial data and device records; the sponsor responsible for each study; the maintenance pathway for already implanted devices; funding for visits and complication management; and which warranties or supplier contracts are assumed, novated or left behind. A court-approved asset sale should include a clear schedule of assumed obligations where patient continuity depends on them.

## What remains unknown

- Which exact obligations toward each participant, clinical site and supplier passed to Science Corporation.
- Whether maintenance, device troubleshooting and non-study clinical follow-up were continuously available at every centre during the transfer.
- Whether the asset-transfer documents addressed legacy warranties or other obligations from Pixium's non-PRIMA products.
- How participants were informed about changes in sponsor, data stewardship and contact points.

## Questions future teams should ask

- Does a technology acquisition explicitly state which trial, data, maintenance and patient-support obligations transfer?
- Is clinical-continuity funding ring-fenced before a cash crisis forces liquidation?
- Can an administrator, hospital or successor identify a responsible contact for each implanted or trial participant after the original company disappears?

## Right of reply

No direct outreach to the insolvency administrator, Science Corporation or participating clinical sites was made for this research pass. This remains an editorial draft, not a legal account of the sale. Before external publication, the transfer documents and current site-level responsibilities should be checked with relevant parties.
