# ATI / Pulsante: shutdown, support gap and technology transfer

This case distinguishes the clinical evidence for an implanted therapy from the survival of its manufacturer and the later transfer of intellectual property. The technology transfer is documented; complete continuity of support is not.

## Case identity and outcome

Autonomic Technologies (ATI) developed the Pulsante system, an implanted sphenopalatine ganglion (SPG) stimulator for on-demand treatment of chronic cluster-headache attacks. A randomized sham-controlled study reported pain relief for some attacks, while the company itself later ceased operating. [S-ATI-01] [S-ATI-02]

**Documented fact:** A patent record shows a transfer chain from ATI through an assignment for the benefit of creditors to Unity HA, followed by a recorded change of name to Realeve. Realeve's own December 2020 announcement said Unity HA had acquired ATI's intellectual property. This establishes a technology transfer; it does not establish that the buyer assumed every clinical or maintenance duty. [S-ATI-03] [S-ATI-04]

**Attributed testimony:** People interviewed by Nature after ATI's closure described difficulty obtaining proprietary software and replacement components. In 2022 Realeve's then-chief executive told Nature that it did not yet have FDA or CE authorization to market the successor therapy or provide support, while the interim chief executive said Denmark had authorized distribution of replacement systems and software and expected deliveries later in 2023. [S-ATI-02]

**Unknown:** The public sources cited do not establish whether the planned Danish deliveries occurred or what support services Realeve currently provides to individual legacy users. Its current website claims that the system has been implanted in more than 700 patients, but does not identify the evidence behind the number or describe a legacy-support program. [S-ATI-05]

## What was built and tested

The Pulsante implant was placed near the SPG through an oral surgical approach. A patient held an external controller against the cheek to power and activate stimulation for an attack. The Pathway CH-1 randomized study assessed acute pain relief; its primary endpoint was pain relief at 15 minutes after stimulation. Its report showed a difference between full stimulation and sham for the attack-level outcome, but it was a small study and attack-level results should not be read as a guarantee of individual clinical benefit. [S-ATI-01]

The Nature report described European authorization to market the device in 2012 and continuing US trials. That European regulatory history did not ensure that an operating company, proprietary adjustment software and replacement parts would remain available indefinitely. [S-ATI-02]

**Editorial inference:** The case illustrates that a patent or product-technology transfer can preserve a development path without immediately restoring clinical support. The Nature article documented a period when recipients still lacked a replacement route, while the company described regulatory constraints and later Danish authorization. The reviewed evidence does not determine what happened in every jurisdiction or after the article. [S-ATI-02] [S-ATI-03]

## Cost of the assumption

The patient accounts reported by Nature describe a device that had become important to managing severe, recurring pain. One user relied on engineering skills to replace a battery in an external controller after the original maker's support ended; another sought assistance from Realeve. These are attributed individual experiences, not a survey of all users. [S-ATI-02]

Regulatory authorization, technical transfer, manufacturing readiness and patient-level support were separate issues. The device's breakthrough designation, reported in the article, was not marketing authorization. FDA explains that Breakthrough Device designation provides a development and review program; it does not remove the need to meet marketing-authorization requirements. [S-ATI-02] [S-ATI-07]

No direct right-of-reply request was made for this release. Corporate statements and patient accounts are attributed; no claim is made about motive or legal responsibility.

## What remains unknown

- The exact date ATI ceased operating and the complete insolvency chronology.
- Which entity held each operating asset, software tool, patient record and support obligation at each date.
- Whether Realeve completed the planned deliveries of replacement devices and software.
- The geographic scope and current availability of support for legacy implants.
- Whether the current website's figure of more than 700 implants counts historic ATI use, Realeve-era use, or both.

## What the evidence suggests future teams should do differently

Treat service continuity as a distinct transfer deliverable. The case supports asking whether the next owner receives usable programming tools, regulated replacement parts, clinical records, trained personnel and funds to support implanted users before a replacement product can be marketed.

## Questions future teams should ask

- When intellectual property changes hands, which practical support assets and obligations transfer with it?
- Can a legacy user obtain replacement external components before a successor device is approved?
- Who can adjust the implant if proprietary software and trained staff disappear?
- Does a regulatory pathway for future commercialization also provide a lawful route to care for existing users?
