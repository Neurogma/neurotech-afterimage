## Case identity and outcome

Optobionics developed the Artificial Silicon Retina (ASR), a photovoltaic subretinal microchip intended to stimulate surviving retinal cells in people with retinitis pigmentosa. A six-patient pilot study published in 2004 reported electrically functioning implants during 6–18 months of follow-up and visual-function observations that the authors said required further study. Specialist reporting and later literature state that the original company ceased operating in May 2007 and was described at the time as being in bankruptcy. A legacy website associated with founder Alan Chow says he later acquired the Optobionics name and ASR implants and intended to reorganize a new company. The defensible outcome is **original-company cessation and development discontinuity, with the successor pathway and recipient-level support unresolved**. [S-ASR-01]–[S-ASR-04]

## What the public record shows

The peer-reviewed pilot enrolled six patients with retinitis pigmentosa. The paper reported that the implants functioned electrically over 6–18 months, with no observed rejection, infection, inflammation, erosion, neovascularization, retinal detachment or migration during that limited follow-up. It also described visual-function changes and explicitly called for additional research to assess and verify outcomes. The small, early study does not establish generalizable efficacy, lifetime safety or a standard clinical pathway. [S-ASR-01]

A 2007 specialist report discussed phase II observations and described the company as being in bankruptcy, with phase III development depending on a purchaser continuing the programme. A subsequent review states that the original company ceased operations in May 2007. The legacy company website says the founder acquired the Optobionics name and implants for a proposed reorganization and claims the technology had been implanted in 42 patients. That number is a company-originated claim and was not independently confirmed here from a complete participant registry. The website does not establish that the proposed successor resumed manufacturing, conducted new trials or formally assumed every participant-support obligation. [S-ASR-02]–[S-ASR-04]

## What was built

The ASR was described as a silicon chip approximately 2 mm in diameter, with around 5,000 microphotodiodes and electrode tips. It harvested incident light to generate electrical stimulation within the subretinal space. The programme also explored a proposed neurotrophic effect, but early visual-function observations in a very small cohort were not definitive proof of treatment effect. [S-ASR-01] [S-ASR-04]

## Chronology

- **2002–2003:** Six participants received an ASR implant in one eye for the pilot study; the fellow eye served as a control. [S-ASR-01]
- **April 2004:** The initial human study was published, reporting implant electrical function during 6–18 months of follow-up and calling for further research. [S-ASR-01]
- **May 2007:** The original Optobionics company was reported to have ceased operations; specialist reporting described the company as in bankruptcy. [S-ASR-02] [S-ASR-03]
- **After original-company cessation:** The legacy website said founder Alan Chow acquired the name and ASR implants and would reorganize a new company. The extent of any subsequent operational or clinical continuity is not independently established. [S-ASR-04]

## What was assumed

The public sources do not establish a single cause of the company's cessation or allow the business outcome to be attributed to a clinical failure. **Editorial inference:** early human signals were not enough to guarantee the resources required for larger trials, regulatory work, manufacturing, or long-term follow-up. The distinction between a founder's asset/name acquisition and an operational successor with patient-support capacity is particularly important here.

## Consequences

The pilot and the original company's cessation are documented. The sources cited do not establish the condition of each implant in the long term, the number of patients still monitored after the published follow-up, whether there were later explants or complications, or whether a successor provided support at scale. The company's claim of 42 implants is explicitly attributed rather than treated as an independently verified population count. [S-ASR-01]–[S-ASR-04]

## What the evidence suggests future teams should do differently

**Editorial inference:** experimental implant trials should establish the custodian of implant identifiers, operative records, adverse-event history and future clinical follow-up before first implantation. A transfer of the company name, patents or physical implants should not be represented as a transfer of clinical obligations unless records specify the duties assumed, available funding, responsible clinicians and access to technical files.

## What remains unknown

- The complete number of implanted recipients and their longer-term clinical outcomes.
- The precise legal form of the original company's closure and full insolvency/asset records.
- Whether the subsequent name/asset acquisition included regulatory files, manufacturing processes, clinical records and formal follow-up obligations.
- Whether the proposed successor ever resumed development or established an ongoing clinical-support service.
- Long-term complication, revision and explant rates beyond the limited published follow-up.

## Questions future teams should ask

- Who is responsible for follow-up and adverse-event handling when an experimental implant sponsor becomes insolvent?
- Does an asset transfer include technical records, implant registries and funding for future clinical care, or only intellectual property and physical assets?
- Are early pilot observations clearly distinguished from proof of safety and efficacy for wider clinical use?

## Right of reply

No outreach was made to Optobionics, its founder, the original investigators or the centres involved. The founder-associated website is an interested-party source and should not be treated as independent evidence of a functioning successor. Relevant parties should be invited to clarify the present status of the implanted cohort before external publication.
