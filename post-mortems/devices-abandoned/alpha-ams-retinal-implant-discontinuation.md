## Case identity and outcome

Alpha IMS and Alpha AMS were subretinal visual prostheses developed by Retina Implant AG for people with profound sight loss from retinal degeneration. The documented outcome is **the original manufacturer’s dissolution and discontinuation of Alpha AMS production and supply in March 2019**. This is a lifecycle and translation case. It does not mean that the devices never provided useful visual perception, nor that all implants stopped functioning on the day manufacture ended.

## What the public record shows

The public record documents both limited clinical function and a later supply discontinuity. A prospective study described selected visual functions in a small implanted cohort, but a 2019 sector report stated that Retina Implant AG's production and hospital supply had ceased; the company's dissolution notice separately reported the shareholders' decision to dissolve the entity. The sources support a distinction between clinical performance and ongoing commercial availability, but do not establish uninterrupted support for every recipient. [S-RA-01] [S-RA-02] [S-RA-03]

### What was built and tested

The Alpha AMS system aimed to restore limited visual perception by electrically stimulating retinal neurons. In a prospective 15-person study with 12-month follow-up, 13 participants had implant-mediated light perception and localization; the paper also reported improvement on some everyday tasks in 13 of 15 participants. Visual acuity remained severely limited and four participants experienced serious adverse events. These are clinically meaningful but qualified findings; they do not establish normal sight or durable commercial viability. [S-RA-02]

### Chronology and decision

- **2017 — prospective Alpha AMS report.** The study followed 15 people for 12 months, reported functional outcomes in selected tasks and serious adverse events, and described the device as CE-certified and reimbursed in Germany at that time. Clinical performance and commercial sustainability are separate questions. [S-RA-02]
- **March 2019 — manufacturing and supply discontinue.** A contemporaneous ophthalmology-sector article reported that Alpha AMS production and supply to hospitals had stopped and that Retina Implant AG had concluded business activities. It stated that clinical partners would continue care for people already implanted. That report is not proof that every centre had uninterrupted support or that all future replacement needs could be met. [S-RA-03]
- **April 2019 — dissolution announcement.** BioRegio STERN published a company-sourced notice saying shareholders had decided on 19 March to dissolve Retina Implant AG. The statement described the company’s assessment that the anticipated medical progress had not materialized sufficiently, alongside regulatory and health-system challenges. This is the company’s explanation, not an independently established causal decomposition. [S-RA-01]

## What was assumed

No internal commercial plan or decision minutes were reviewed, so no expectation is attributed to Retina Implant AG. **Editorial inference:** a reasonable clinical proof-of-concept and some reimbursement at a given time do not ensure that manufacturing, supply and long-term service can be sustained. The company’s own explanation points to a gap between anticipated and realized medical progress, but this does not quantify the contribution of each clinical, economic or regulatory factor.

## What the evidence suggests future teams should do differently

**Editorial inference:** an implant can meet technical and biological milestones and provide limited benefit to some recipients while still failing to sustain manufacturing, supply or a viable commercial route. For a high-cost implant with a small eligible population, the post-market plan must include replacement and revision components, specialised follow-up, financing of long-term care and a transition path if the manufacturer dissolves.

Teams should define clinically meaningful outcomes in everyday activities before launch and report the distribution of benefit—not only whether stimulation produces a percept. Commercial milestones should include a funded manufacturing runway and a credible plan for implanted recipients even if new sales stop. Regulators, payers and implanting centres should avoid treating certification as a guarantee of indefinite supply.

## Consequences

The sources support that useful but limited visual functions were reported in a small cohort, that serious adverse events occurred, and that manufacturer activity/product supply ended. The sources do not demonstrate a universal lack of benefit, a single proven cause for closure, or the present support status of every implant recipient. No claim is made that a replacement manufacturer assumed all original obligations.

## What remains unknown

- The complete list of implanted recipients and their outcomes after business activities ceased.
- Actual continuity of parts, revision procedures, device expertise and clinical follow-up at each centre.
- The full commercial, regulatory and health-system contribution to the dissolution decision.
- Whether any specific technology or IP transfer retained obligations to existing recipients.

## Questions future teams should ask

- What proportion of recipients gains a measurable improvement on prespecified daily activities, and how durable is the benefit?
- Who pays for and supplies essential components after production ends?
- Are clinical partners equipped to maintain follow-up without manufacturer engineering support?
- Does the company’s explanation for closure have independent corroboration or remain an attributed account?
