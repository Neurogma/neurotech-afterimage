## Case identity and outcome

Freehand was an implanted neuromuscular stimulation system designed to help some people with tetraplegia grasp, hold and release objects. The evidence supports a meaningful distinction between clinical capability demonstrated in selected users and the later availability of the original system. This dossier classifies the outcome as **product availability and support discontinuity**, not a demonstrated technical defect.

**Documented fact:** FDA’s PMA record establishes approval for the intended C5/C6 spinal-cord-injury population and lists the implant receiver-stimulator, electrode set and external control system. Later specialist review material says the first-generation system was no longer available from NeuroControl, while describing selected-centre access/experience. Nature’s 2022 retrospective reports a substantial support and spare-parts problem for some recipients. Those latter descriptions are secondary accounts, not a census of every user. [S-FH-01] [S-FH-03] [S-SC-06]

## What the public record shows

The public record first establishes a regulated intended use: FDA approval in 1997 covered an implanted upper-extremity neuroprosthesis for selected people with C5/C6 tetraplegia to help grasp, hold and release objects. A small clinical report described use and functional gains among selected participants; a later specialist review reports that the first-generation system was no longer available from NeuroControl and that access was selective at some centres. This supports a lifecycle and availability concern, not a claim that every implanted system became unusable or unsupported at the same time. [S-FH-01] [S-FH-02] [S-FH-03]

### What was built

The Freehand system stimulated forearm muscles through implanted electrodes and used an external controller to coordinate grasp functions. The FDA granted PMA approval on 15 August 1997. This regulatory milestone establishes that a device was approved for the specified indication; it does not establish the scale of commercial distribution or guarantee permanent manufacturing and service. [S-FH-01]

### Clinical use and product lifecycle

- **1997 — FDA approval.** The approval record identifies BioControl Technology Inc. and the system’s intended indication and components. The FDA’s public record and summary histories do not amount to evidence of widespread or durable commercial supply. One FDA summary describes limited marketing history, so approval should not be presented as proof that the system was broadly sold. [S-FH-01]
- **2002 — clinical report.** Taylor and colleagues reported functional use among a small cohort: seven of nine participants were described as daily users, with improvements on certain tasks and activities of daily living. Those results support the system’s potential for selected patients, not population-wide benefit or guaranteed long-term durability. [S-FH-02]
- **Later years — availability narrows.** SCIRE’s specialist evidence review states that the first-generation Freehand was no longer available from NeuroControl and notes that devices were available on a selective basis in several centres. Nature’s later reporting describes users seeking information and spare parts after company support ended or became unavailable. These sources may refer to different periods, regions and forms of support; they do not establish the situation of every recipient. [S-FH-03] [S-SC-06]

## What was assumed

The public record does not reveal the original manufacturer's internal lifecycle assumptions. **Editorial inference:** FDA approval, clinical use or selected-centre expertise could be mistaken for assurance of long-term component and manufacturer support. The available evidence separates these concepts: approval and selected-user function are documented, whereas broad durable supply and the present support status for every recipient are not.

## What the evidence suggests future teams should do differently

**Editorial inference:** an implant’s lifecycle is a multi-decade commitment even when the original product was distributed narrowly. Commercial availability, research access, active follow-up at selected centres, spare-parts supply and manufacturer-backed technical support are separate states. A case can show an important support risk even if the device delivered function and was not shown to be defective.

Before implanting a device, teams should publish a support-continuity plan with replacement-component availability, device programming and extraction capability, clinical expertise, maintenance of external controllers, data preservation, manufacturer insolvency triggers, and a funded transition plan. Patients and centres need a clear route to confirm whether a unit remains supported, research-only, serviceable by a designated centre, or no longer maintainable.

## Consequences

Clinical publication supports daily use and improved selected functions in a small group. Later sources report loss or reduction of commercial and technical support pathways. The reviewed evidence does not quantify how many users experienced each problem, whether every device ceased to function, or whether every user lacked access to a specialist centre. It also does not resolve the full mismatch between FDA approval records and descriptions of limited market availability.

## What remains unknown

- The total number of devices implanted, actively used and still serviceable over time.
- Current support status at each specialist centre and availability of replacement components.
- Whether any successor formally assumed manufacturer, warranty, or patient-support obligations.
- How many recipients experienced interruption, what the duration was, and which practical effects followed.
- The exact chronology and geography of commercial availability; public sources describe market history differently.

## Questions future teams should ask

- Is there an identified entity funded to support every implanted system after the original company exits?
- Are critical external controllers, leads, consumables and programming tools reproducible?
- Who can safely reprogram, troubleshoot, revise or explant a legacy system when the original team is gone?
- How are patients and implanting clinicians informed when support status changes?
