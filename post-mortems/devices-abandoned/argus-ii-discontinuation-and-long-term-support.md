# Argus II: discontinuation and long-term support

Argus II provided limited artificial visual perception to some people with profound vision loss. This case concerns product discontinuation and a later support interruption; it does not claim that recipients regained natural sight and then uniformly became blind again.

## Case identity and outcome

Argus II was an epiretinal prosthesis for people with severe-to-profound retinitis pigmentosa and outer-retinal degeneration. A camera on glasses sent information through an external processor to an implanted electrode array, which stimulated viable retinal cells. FDA approved it under a Humanitarian Device Exemption in February 2013. [S-AR-01] [S-AR-02]

**Documented fact:** In May 2019 Second Sight said it would suspend production of new systems, reduce commercial activity and redirect resources toward Orion, while stating that it would continue support for existing Argus users. The company's later SEC reporting described the system as discontinued following a reassessment of commercial considerations and reported approximately 350 recipients worldwide. [S-AR-03] [S-AR-04]

**Attributed testimony:** A 2022 IEEE Spectrum investigation documented users who experienced device shutdowns or other loss of function and included Second Sight's statement that its reduced workforce could not maintain the previous level of support and communication. Those reports describe individual users, not all recipients. [S-AR-05]

**Unknown:** The SEC filings reviewed establish that a successor entity, Cortigent, continued operating legacy programs through a transition support agreement funded by Vivani through the end of 2024. They do not specify the post-2024 service level for each Argus II user. [S-AR-04]

## What was built and what clinical evidence established

Argus II was intended to provide artificial visual perception, not ordinary or high-resolution sight. In the pivotal prospective study, 30 participants with profound blindness were followed; the study assessed visual function, functional vision, safety and device reliability. Its five-year publication reported improvement on selected visual tasks and described an overall safety and benefit signal. The study was small and single-arm, so it did not establish the same benefit for every recipient or compare Argus II with a control group. [S-AR-02] [S-AR-06]

The FDA's HDE pathway allowed marketing for a rare condition based on probable benefit with an acceptable risk profile. That regulatory status is not equivalent to proof of broad effectiveness for all forms of blindness. [S-AR-01]

The official 2019 promise and later reports of an interrupted support level are both material. They refer to different times and different kinds of evidence: an announced plan versus later accounts of actual user and clinic experience. [S-AR-03] [S-AR-05]

## Patient impact

Published recipient accounts described the system as useful for some tasks, while also emphasizing coarse, artificial percepts and considerable variation. In one reported case, a user's Argus II system shut down and the light-and-dark percepts she had used were lost. This is evidence about that user; it is not evidence that all recipients had the same benefit, device status or outcome. [S-AR-05]

Further commercialization stopping did not automatically turn off every implant or remove every benefit. Some systems remained functional for years, while individual devices and external components could fail. Loss of technical support matters because troubleshooting, compatible replacement components and experienced clinical teams may be needed to preserve function. [S-AR-04] [S-AR-05]

## What remains unknown

- How many Argus II systems were functional or in use at each point after 2019.
- Whether each recipient could access repairs, replacement external equipment and clinical programming during the support interruption.
- What patient-support services Cortigent offered after Vivani's transition funding ended in 2024.
- Whether the commercial outcome would have differed with a larger indication or a different support and reimbursement model.

No direct right-of-reply request was made. The dossier cites the company's 2019 support commitment and the later published company statement; it does not claim to know each recipient's present support status.

## What the evidence suggests future teams should do differently

Plan for long-term support as part of the device's clinical design and economics. A system with an implant and proprietary external components creates dependencies that outlast the sales period. The record supports this as a continuity lesson; it does not establish that support costs alone caused Argus II's discontinuation.

## Questions future teams should ask

- Who can maintain external hardware and software after production stops?
- Are replacement parts and programming tools available to independent qualified clinics?
- How will each user be notified if the support level changes?
- Does the product's business plan account for the rare-disease population and years of follow-up?
